\begin{abstract}
Cryptocurrency exchanges must flag laundering transactions, but confirmed illicit labels are scarce and late, while licit ones are cheap.
On the Elliptic Bitcoin graph (203,769 transactions, 49 time steps) we show that illicit transactions are \emph{not} outliers: only 1.7\% exceed a 3$\sigma$ rule versus 22.3\% of licit ones, so one-class detectors rank them \emph{below} normal (test ROC-AUC 0.17).
We therefore train a positive-unlabeled detector (\ours{}) that separates licit from unlabeled transactions and never sees an illicit label.
On a chronological test split, \ours{} reaches PR-AUC 0.546\,$\pm$\,0.024 (8.4$\times$ prevalence) and 82\% precision in its top 100 alerts, statistically indistinguishable in PR-AUC from a supervised GraphSAGE network and recovering 69\% of supervised XGBoost's PR-AUC.
Ablations show that 1\% illicit contamination of the licit labels cuts this PR-AUC by 74\%, and every method fails after a dark-market shutdown.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\paragraph{Scenario and stakeholders.}
Virtual-asset service providers must screen the transactions they touch for money laundering.
Every alert goes to a compliance analyst, who decides whether to freeze funds and file a suspicious-activity report.
A missed laundering transaction (FN) exposes the provider to regulatory penalties; a false alert (FP) costs analyst time from a fixed review budget.
Licit labels come from the provider's own KYC-verified customers and from counterparties attributed to regulated exchanges; analyst capacity fixes the alert budget per period.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Supervised detectors work well on Bitcoin data \citep{weber2019aml} but need confirmed illicit labels, which arrive late.
One-class models, treating anomalies as rare far-away points \citep{chandola2009survey}, are the label-free default, yet \citet{lorenz2020label} found them poor and illicit instances not outlying; we quantify, explain and exploit this.

\paragraph{Definition of anomaly.}
Our data belong to the \textbf{Graph} category: the unit of detection is one Bitcoin transaction, a node in a payment graph.
It is anomalous if Elliptic attributes it to an illicit entity (scams, malware, ransomware, Ponzi schemes) and normal if to a licit one (exchanges, wallet providers, miners).
Example normal sample: tx 378120377 (step 16, licit; \texttt{local\_53}$=0.92$, \texttt{local\_55}$=0.45$, in/out degree 0/2).
Example anomaly: tx 232014511 (step 1, illicit; \texttt{local\_53}$=-0.49$, \texttt{local\_55}$=-0.47$, degree 1/1).

\paragraph{Research question.}
\emph{Can a detector fitted without any illicit label rank illicit Bitcoin transactions above licit ones on future time steps, even though illicit transactions are not outliers?}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(C1) Illicit transactions are less extreme than licit ones (1.7\% vs.\ 22.3\% beyond 3$\sigma$), which inverts outlier detectors (test ROC-AUC 0.17 for Isolation Forest, 0.33 for 3$\sigma$; \S\ref{sec:eda}, \S\ref{sec:results}).
(C2) A licit-vs-unlabeled (\ours{}) detector reaches test PR-AUC 0.546 and P@100 0.82 without illicit labels: far above label-free baselines, indistinguishable in PR-AUC from a supervised GNN, below supervised trees (\S\ref{sec:results}).
(C3) It is robust to the unlabeled pool size but collapses when 1\% of licit labels are wrong (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).
(C4) 42\% of its misses fall after a dark-market shutdown, a blind spot shared with supervised models (\S\ref{sec:analysis}).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Data category and literature.}
Our data are in the \textbf{Graph} category (transactions are nodes, payment flows directed edges); its key challenge is multi-hop structure, as laundering routes funds through chains of transactions.
\citet{weber2019aml} released Elliptic and compared supervised models on a 70:30 temporal split (Table~\ref{tab:related}); Random Forest on all features beat graph convolutional networks.
EvolveGCN \citep{pareja2020evolvegcn} models the graph sequence over time (illicit F1 0.720 in \citealp{weber2019aml}) and reports that no model recovers after the dark-market shutdown.
\citet{lorenz2020label} found seven unsupervised detectors at illicit F1 ${\le}$0.19 and matched supervision with active learning on 5\% of the labels.
Positive-unlabeled (PU) learning trains a classifier from one labeled class plus unlabeled data \citep{elkan2008pu,bekker2020pu}; we do not use Deep SAD \citep{ruff2020deepsad}, which trains on labeled anomalies that a provider without confirmed cases lacks.

\paragraph{Positioning and method selection.}
Tier~1 assumes anomalies are extreme in some feature (3$\sigma$) or nothing (random score).
Tier~2 assumes illicit mass is over-represented in unlabeled relative to licit data (\ours{}); the one-class Isolation Forest \citep{liu2008iforest} instead assumes anomalies are easy to isolate.
Tier~3 is fully supervised: Random Forest \citep{breiman2001rf} as in \citet{weber2019aml}, XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost}, and a GNN.
Graph context enters as one-/two-hop aggregates and degree (A4) and through GCN \citep{kipf2017gcn} or GraphSAGE \citep{hamilton2017graphsage} message passing, both supervised (T3c) and inside \ours{} (T2$''$).
As unknown nodes dominate neighbourhoods (\S\ref{sec:eda}), we expect a node-level PU classifier to suit this graph. Unlike prior work, we fit without illicit labels, explain \emph{why} outlier detectors invert, and analyse failures per transaction.

\section{Task Definition and Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Data set.}
We use the Elliptic Data Set \citep{weber2019aml,elliptic2019data} (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0; an unmodified copy is in our repository).
It contains $N=203{,}769$ transactions and 234,355 edges in 49 time steps about two weeks apart; no edge crosses time steps (verified: 0 of 234,355), so graph features never mix periods.
Each node has $D=166$ anonymised features: the time step, 93 local features (LF; e.g.\ fees, input/output counts) and 72 one-hop neighbour aggregates (AF); we add in/out degree and two-hop aggregates (mean of the neighbours' AF). Unit and examples: \S1.

\paragraph{Labels.}
Heuristic entity-level labelling \citep{weber2019aml} gives 4,545 illicit, 42,019 licit and 157,205 \emph{unknown} transactions; labels may be noisy, and unknown ones (both classes) are never evaluated but serve \ours{} as unlabeled data.
The paradigm is semi-supervised (PU): licit and unlabeled data for fitting, illicit labels only for validation-based selection.

\paragraph{Class imbalance.}
Overall, 2.2\% of transactions are illicit; among labeled ones the ratio is 9.8\% vs.\ 90.2\% (1\,:\,9.2), and on the test split 6.5\% (1\,:\,14.4).
``Always licit'' reaches 93.5\% test accuracy with zero recall, so we report PR-AUC and tuned-$\tau$ metrics, never accuracy or a 0.5 cut-off; as licit rows dominate an unweighted log-loss, every $\tau$ is tuned on validation (GNNs use a class-balanced loss).

\label{sec:eda}

All statistics use the training split only (Figure~\ref{fig:eda}).

(b) Cumulative share of the largest $|z|$-score (w.r.t.\ licit statistics): illicit transactions are \emph{less} extreme than licit ones; dashed line = 3$\sigma$. (c) PCA fitted on licit data: illicit points form a compact cluster inside the region occupied by licit ones.}
\label{fig:eda}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Distribution contrast.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eda}a shows \texttt{local\_53}, the feature with the largest Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) distance between classes (0.66, mutual information 0.21 nats).
Most illicit transactions sit on one value ($-0.49\pm0.001$: 77\% of illicit, 16\% of licit training transactions); licit values spread widely.
Figure~\ref{fig:eda}b is the key observation: only 1.7\% of illicit transactions have any local feature beyond 3$\sigma$ of the licit distribution, against 22.3\% of licit transactions.
In PCA space (Figure~\ref{fig:eda}c), illicit points form one compact region.

\paragraph{Separability and redundancy.}
Local features separate the classes better than one-hop aggregates (mean KS 0.38 vs.\ 0.27; 63 vs.\ 30 features with KS $>0.3$; mean MI 0.088 vs.\ 0.060 nats) and than degree or two-hop aggregates (mean KS 0.20, 0.18).
89 feature pairs have $|r|>0.95$, and licit behaviour drifts moderately within training (mean KS 0.11, steps 1--14 vs.\ 15--29).
On training edges, 56\% of the labeled neighbours of illicit transactions are illicit (5$\times$ the base rate), but 59\% of all their neighbours are unknown (licit: 46\%).

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
\textbf{H1}: Outlier-based scores (3$\sigma$, Isolation Forest, Mahalanobis) will rank illicit transactions \emph{below} licit ones.
\textbf{H2}: A model that contrasts licit data with unlabeled data will rank them above, because the compact illicit mode is denser in the unlabeled pool than among labeled licit transactions.
\textbf{H3}: Graph context (aggregates, degree, message passing) will add little, as unknown neighbours dominate.

\paragraph{Pipeline.}
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the six checkpoints of the Tier~2 pipeline.
The input is the 93 local features as released (provider-scaled per feature, to which tree rankings are invariant); the time step is dropped so the model cannot memorise periods.
In the training steps, labeled licit transactions get target $t=0$ and unknown ones $t=1$ (23,510 vs.\ 94,423 rows); illicit training rows are \emph{not} used.
An XGBoost classifier (300 trees, depth 6, learning rate 0.1, row/column subsampling 0.8) learns $\hat{P}(t{=}1\mid x)$.

\paragraph{Anomaly score.}
For a well-calibrated classifier, $s(x)=\hat{P}(t{=}1\mid x)$ is monotone in the density ratio $p_{\mathcal{U}}(x)/p_{\mathcal{L}}(x)$.
If the unknown pool is a mixture $p_{\mathcal{U}}=\pi\,p_{\text{ill}}+(1-\pi)\,p_{\text{lic}}$ and labeled licit data follow $p_{\text{lic}}$, the ratio equals $(1-\pi)+\pi\,p_{\text{ill}}(x)/p_{\text{lic}}(x)$ and ranks transactions by how much likelier they are under the illicit than the licit distribution \citep{elkan2008pu}, rewarding a dense illicit mode that licit data rarely reach (H2).

\paragraph{Threshold and error costs.}
An FN lets laundered funds pass (regulatory risk); an FP costs analyst time.
With cost ratio $r=c_{\mathrm{FN}}/c_{\mathrm{FP}}$, the cost-optimal $\tau_r$ minimises $r\cdot\mathrm{FN}+\mathrm{FP}$ on the labeled validation transactions (steps 30--34; the test split is never used).
Tiers are compared at the validation-F1 maximiser $\tau_{\mathrm{F1}}$ ($0.958\pm0.012$ for \ours{}), which weighs both errors about equally ($r\approx1$); deployment should use $\tau_{10}$, which suppresses the costlier FN (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Split and leakage prevention.}
All methods share one chronological split by time step (Table~\ref{tab:data}): train 1--29, validation 30--34, test 35--49, i.e.\ 59/10/31\% of steps and 57/8/36\% of labeled transactions.
Training rows per tier (licit / illicit / unknown, steps 1--29): 3$\sigma$, one-class IF, Mahalanobis and LOF 23,510 / 0 / 0 (normal-only); \ours{} and LvU-GraphSAGE 23,510 / 0 / 94,423 (unknown may hide illicit); RF, XGBoost and supervised GraphSAGE 23,510 / 2,871 / 0.
Every tier tunes on the same 2,922 / 591 validation and is scored on the same 15,587 / 1,083 test transactions; only A1 mixes illicit rows into the licit side.
Detector scalers use licit training rows; the test set is scored once per seed.

\paragraph{Configurations.}
\ours{}: feature set $\in$\{LF, AF, AF+degree\}, depth $\in\{4,6,8\}$ and trees $\in\{100,300\}$, chosen by validation PR-AUC (LF, 6, 300; seed-0 search 0.901, 5-seed mean 0.871).
Isolation Forest: 200 trees, \texttt{max\_samples} $\in\{256,2048\}$ (2048, LF).
RF as in \citet{weber2019aml} (50 trees, 50 features per split, AF); XGBoost as \ours{}, on AF.
GNNs (PyTorch Geometric, \citealp{fey2019pyg}): two GCN or GraphSAGE layers, full-batch, Adam (rate 0.01), early stopping on validation PR-AUC (${\le}$1000 epochs, patience 100); validation picked GraphSAGE, width 64 and AF (the supervised grid also tried width 128).
Depth 4--8 and 100--300 trees span shallow to moderate boosting for ${\sim}10^5$ rows, selected on validation; Tier~3 reuses Weber's RF and \ours{}'s XGBoost settings (on AF; an LF control is in \S\ref{sec:results}).
Stochastic methods use 5 seeds; deterministic ones (3$\sigma$, Mahalanobis, LOF, $k$NN ratio) report a 1,000-resample test bootstrap std ($\dagger$). Software: Python 3.13, scikit-learn 1.9 \citep{pedregosa2011sklearn}, XGBoost 3.4, PyTorch 2.14; hardware: 4 vCPUs, 16\,GB RAM, no GPU.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\textbf{PR-AUC} (primary) ignores the many easy true negatives and suits imbalance \citep{saito2015prc}; its random baseline is the prevalence.
\textbf{P@100}, the precision of the 100 top-scored test transactions, models a fixed analyst budget; \textbf{F1@$\tau$} evaluates the deployed decision; ROC-AUC shows inversion ($<0.5$).

\paragraph{Main comparison.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} supports H1: 3$\sigma$ and the one-class Isolation Forest rank illicit below licit transactions (ROC-AUC 0.33, 0.17) and fall under random PR-AUC.
The 3$\sigma$ rule (fixed $\tau=3$) reaches F1 0.010, and Isolation Forest's val-tuned $\tau$ degenerates to ``alert on everything'' (F1 0.121, as for random scores).
\ours{} supports H2: PR-AUC rises to 0.546\,$\pm$\,0.024 (8.4$\times$ prevalence) and 82\% of its top-100 alerts are illicit.
Bootstrap 95\% CIs of the PR-AUC gap from \ours{}: random [0.45, 0.51], 3$\sigma$ [0.46, 0.53], IF [0.48, 0.54], RF [$-$0.24, $-$0.20], XGBoost [$-$0.27, $-$0.22], GraphSAGE [$-$0.04, 0.03]; paired $t$-tests over seeds agree ($p<10^{-4}$ for every stochastic method except GraphSAGE).

\paragraph{Against Tier 3.}
Supervised trees remain clearly better (XGBoost 0.790, RF 0.767), though \ours{} recovers 69\% of XGBoost's PR-AUC without illicit labels.
The gap is not a feature confound: on LF, as \ours{} uses, RF and XGBoost still reach 0.742 and 0.766 (5 seeds, std $\le$0.003).
It loses because it learns ``unlabeled-like'', not ``illicit'' (verified in \S\ref{sec:analysis}).
RF's F1 varies across seeds (0.42--0.80) unlike its PR-AUC: a $\tau$ tuned at 16.8\% validation prevalence transfers poorly to 6.5\%.
Trees train in ${<}6$\,s, GNNs in 2--5\,min; all score in ${\le}12\,\mu$s per transaction.

\paragraph{Message passing (Graph-specific).}
Supervised GraphSAGE (T3c, PR-AUC 0.552\,$\pm$\,0.035) is indistinguishable from label-free \ours{} ($p=0.79$; CI [$-$0.038, 0.027]) and 0.24 below XGBoost, as trees beat GNNs on Elliptic \citep{weber2019aml}.
Inside \ours{}, message passing fails (T2$''$, 0.189): averaging unknown-dominated neighbourhoods makes most nodes look ``unlabeled'' (H3).

\paragraph{A1: contamination of the normal labels (AD-specific).}
Swapping 1\% of licit training rows for illicit ones (235 rows) cuts PR-AUC from 0.546 to 0.142.
As illicit transactions form one compact mode, a few hundred of them labeled $t=0$ teach the classifier that this mode is ``licit'': \ours{} needs clean licit labels.

\paragraph{A2: size of the unlabeled pool (AD-specific).}
5\% of the unknown pool (4.7k rows) gives the same PR-AUC (0.546); no change is significant (Welch $t$-test over seeds, $p\ge0.36$), so a small unlabeled sample already holds the illicit mode.

\paragraph{A3: scoring function (AD-specific).}
With features fixed (one-class scores fit licit rows, PU scores licit and unlabeled rows), outlier scores invert (Isolation Forest 0.037; Mahalanobis with Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage, \citealp{ledoit2004shrinkage}, 0.038), local density scores (LOF with $k{=}20$, \citealp{breunig2000lof}; a licit/unlabeled 10-NN density ratio) recover some signal (0.19), and the classifier-based density ratio is best (0.546).

\paragraph{A4: graph features (Graph-specific).}
Relative to LF, AF ($+$0.011), AF+degree ($-$0.037) and AF+two-hop ($-$0.010) change test PR-AUC insignificantly (Welch $p\ge0.18$) and lower test F1 (0.30--0.35 vs.\ 0.40), which supports H3; on validation, LF is clearly best (0.871 vs.\ ${\le}$0.783), which is why it was selected.

\paragraph{A5: threshold rule.}
Table~\ref{tab:threshold} shows that $\tau_{\mathrm{F1}}$ behaves like $r\approx1$, and that each step up in $r$ buys recall with more false alerts per catch.
For a compliance provider we recommend fixing $r$ explicitly; with $r=10$ (a miss costs ten reviews), $\tau_{10}$ raises 3,952 alerts (${\approx}$263 per two-week step) for recall 0.75 at 3.9 false alerts per catch.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Temporal failure.}
As in \citet{weber2019aml}, every model fails after a dark market closes at step 43 (Table~\ref{tab:main}): \ours{}'s F1 drops from 0.54 to 0.01 and its PR-AUC from 0.743 to 0.025, the prevalence.
The new illicit population leaves the illicit mode: \texttt{local\_53}$=-0.49$ holds for 84\% of illicit test transactions before step 43 but only 2\% after (training: 77\%), so for the 166 post-shutdown misses this feature contributes $-$0.33 log-odds instead of $+$1.03 for earlier hits.
Steps 43--49 hold 15.6\% of test illicit transactions but 42\% of the 398 misses (seed 0); 69\% of misses are shared by supervised RF, so the blind spot lies in the data.


\paragraph{Failure cases.}
In Table~\ref{tab:cases}, \#1 is a complete inversion ($s-\tau=-0.956$): \texttt{local\_48} (TreeSHAP $-$1.54 log-odds) and \texttt{local\_53}$=1.81$ ($-$1.53), above 99.7\% of illicit but at the 63rd percentile of licit training transactions, pull its score down; RF misses it too (0.04).
\#2 (FN) and \#4 (FP) fall on either side of $\tau=0.97027$ ($s-\tau=-5.1{\times}10^{-4}$ and $+3.8{\times}10^{-5}$): they share 73 of 93 local features (within $10^{-4}$; L2 distance 4.1 vs.\ a median of 10.2 to licit training transactions) yet carry opposite labels.
Label noise or entities reusing one wallet software are plausible but unverifiable causes, as Elliptic anonymises entities.
\#3 is a confident FP ($s=0.99995$) on the illicit mode \texttt{local\_53}$=-0.49$ that RF scores 0.03.
The ``unlabeled-like'' mechanism shows directly: among their 10 nearest training neighbours, licit FPs have 94\% unknown transactions, licit TNs 47\% (pool: 80\%).
\textbf{Shared pattern}: errors stem from overlapping modes and post-shutdown drift, not capacity; the first fix is a few analyst-confirmed labels from the new period (active learning, \citealp{lorenz2020label}).

\paragraph{Interpretability.}
By exact TreeSHAP of \citet{lundberg2020treeshap} (mean $|$SHAP$|$ over test illicit transactions), \texttt{local\_53} (0.88 log-odds), \texttt{local\_47} and \texttt{local\_76} (0.56) dominate: the model uses the illicit mode of \S\ref{sec:eda}.
For the top-scored true positive (tx 162428632, $s=0.99990$, RF 0.94), \texttt{local\_53}$=-0.49$ adds 1.23 log-odds, five other features 0.4--0.9 each.
An analyst escalates when the attribution sits on the known illicit mode and dismisses when a licit counterparty explains it: a reason to review, not proof.

\paragraph{Practical impact.}
At $\tau_{\mathrm{F1}}$, \ours{} raises 2,459 alerts in 15 steps (${\approx}$164 per step) and catches 65\% (${\approx}$704) of 1,083 illicit test transactions; 71\% of alerts are false (2.5 per catch).
With 30 alerts per step (450 in total), \ours{} catches 218 illicit transactions (precision 0.48, 7.4$\times$ random review), supervised RF 239 and XGBoost 245: lacking illicit labels costs 21--27 catches per 450 reviews, so \ours{} suits cold-start triage.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}

\paragraph{Key findings.}
Yes, with qualifications: fitted only on licit and unlabeled transactions, \ours{} ranks future illicit transactions far above chance (PR-AUC 0.546 vs.\ 0.065; P@100 0.82) where outlier detectors invert, because illicit transactions form a compact, non-extreme mode.
It is indistinguishable in PR-AUC from a supervised GraphSAGE and reaches 69\% of XGBoost's, but fails after the dark-market shutdown like every model.
Verdicts: H1 and H2 supported; H3 holds for \ours{} but not for supervised trees (one-hop aggregates add 0.024--0.026).

\paragraph{Reflection and future work.}
The decisive step was testing outlyingness in EDA (Figure~\ref{fig:eda}b), which we would now do first.
Next: (i) after a drift alarm, query analyst labels for top-scored unlabeled transactions to fix post-shutdown misses; (ii) estimate the unlabeled class prior $\pi$ \citep{bekker2020pu} to calibrate $\tau$ across periods.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\emph{Data coverage}: one blockchain, 49 steps, heuristic labels, anonymised features (with real ones we would add address-cluster age and velocity); 77\% of transactions are unlabeled, so scores refer to labeled ones only.
\emph{Assumptions}: \ours{} assumes clean licit labels (A1 shows 1\% noise is fatal) and that the unlabeled pool contains the illicit modes of the future, which fails after the shutdown.
\emph{Graph}: deeper or temporal GNNs (e.g.\ EvolveGCN) are untested.
\emph{Generalisation}: extreme fraud (e.g.\ one-off thefts) may not invert.

\paragraph{Deployment considerations.}
\emph{Concept drift}: illicit behaviour changed abruptly at step 43; audit the top 30 alerts per step and retrain when their precision falls below half of its deployment value, as scores alone do not reveal a collapse.
\emph{Inputs}: the unlabeled pool is every other on-chain transaction; 30 alerts per step is ${\approx}$3 reviews per working day.
\emph{Latency, privacy}: ${\approx}1.5\,\mu$s per transaction; linking addresses to people is regulated.
